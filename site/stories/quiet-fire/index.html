The quiet fire

What happens to a flame when gravity goes away, and could a harmful fire be hard to notice? This story follows the experiments and marks where the answers stop.

Partly data

Each observation comes from a flight experiment or a NASA document, but none tested whether a dim or invisible fire would go unnoticed on a spacecraft, so that idea is our reading of separate pieces.

01 of 07

A flame you can barely see

On the Mir station, a candle's flame in weightlessness was nearly invisible to the naked eye.1 It burned slowly, yet it burned down to a lower oxygen level than the identical candle would on Earth.1

A NASA summary says smoke detectors would be unable to sense these kinds of weak, non-smoking flames, but adds that they are so weak as to be non-hazardous.2 So what else changes without gravity? And could a harmful fire be as hard to notice?

02 of 07

No up, no draught

On Earth, as hot gas rises from the flame, gravity pulls cooler, denser air in at the bottom of it.3 NASA's fire-protection brief says that in microgravity this flow does not occur.3

Another candle, lit in weightlessness, began as a spherical, bright yellow flame.4 After 8 to 10 seconds the yellow disappeared, and the flame became blue and nearly hemispherical.4

03 of 07

What the air flow does to a fire

On a spacecraft, NASA's fire-protection brief says, ventilation fans replace natural convection and can supply the air a fire needs to burn.6 In the BASS-II experiments, flames spread over black PMMA (acrylic) rods in weightlessness at oxygen levels as low as 17.5 percent.7 On the ground, spread was not seen below 19 percent.7

The report says this is probably due to slower air flowing against the flame in the weightless tests.7 In one test on a 100-micrometre PMMA film, the flame went out within 2 seconds of the flow being cut off.8 Yet in one Saffire V test on thin cotton fabric, the fan was off for 70 seconds, but the flame grew back when the flow returned.9

Both flamelets and embers were present when imaging stopped, so the authors cannot tell whether the flame survived the next 60 seconds.10 The Saffire flights aimed to observe fires at a range of atmospheres, including those expected for exploration missions.11

04 of 07

The stage you cannot see

On the space station, a burning droplet of heptane fuel appeared to go out but kept burning with no visible flame.1213 NASA says the fire went out twice, and that the second stage was sustained by what is known as cool flame chemical heat release.1213

In one decane droplet test, the colour and ultraviolet cameras were not sensitive enough to register any flame image in the cool stage.14 Radiometers, which measure radiated heat, still recorded heat from the droplet, which the authors read as active chemical heat release.14

05 of 07

Smoke that does not rise

In space, smoke does not rise, so detectors sit inside the ventilation system.15 In about 20 years of Space Shuttle operations, five potential fire-causing incidents (overheating or electrical short circuits) occurred, and in none was the early fire signal strong enough to set off a smoke detector.16

In the Saffire VI experiments, the smoke from the fire event was readily detected.17 But a typical alarm threshold was reached only at 981 seconds, and by then, the authors say, most of the hazardous gas release had occurred.17

06 of 07

In one test, the gases stood out more than the heat

In the Saffire VI tests, the authors found the rise in temperature and pressure less significant than the rise in carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide.18 The paper says the fires were generally 1.5 to 3 kilowatts, which it calls a small fire by terrestrial standards.19 In two PMMA tests, carbon monoxide rose by 308 and 320 parts per million (ppm), well above the 24-hour SMAC (100 ppm) and quite close to the 1-hour level (425 ppm), the authors say.20

07 of 07

What we cannot yet say

Among the sources we checked, none reports a cool flame on a solid material or a cargo item.21 In 2001, a NASA ISS materials manager listed a conundrum: air flow is needed to transport smoke to traditional sensors, yet air flow is the worst thing for microgravity fires.22

What this story does not show

  • Candle and droplet results are not results for cargo.
  • The Saffire VI fire tests in this story ran in an uncrewed spacecraft, not in a crewed cabin.
  • No test here asked whether a person or an alarm would miss a dim fire; that idea is our reading of separate results.
  • Nothing here is measured for the Moon or Mars.
  • No sentence here is a verdict on any material or item.
