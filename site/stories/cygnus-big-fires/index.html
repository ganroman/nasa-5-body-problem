What did the big Cygnus fires show?

What did the large Saffire fires show about how a fire grows and whether it stays out? This story reads the results and marks where the answers stop.

Data

Each observation comes from a Saffire flight test or a NASA paper on it. Where we say what to take from them, that is our reading and is marked so.

01 of 06

Fires lit on purpose

The Saffire flights aimed to observe the growth and spread of realistic fires in low gravity over thin and thick solid fuel samples at a range of atmospheric conditions, including the current and anticipated environments for exploration missions.1 What did they do once they were burning?

02 of 06

A thick fuel, a front that stays put

Two concurrent PMMA (acrylic) samples, IV-2 and V-3, did not spread appreciably.2 They stayed fixed at the leading edge of the sample and advanced only through surface regression.2 The authors call this typical for thick fuels in low gravity, and different from what is seen in normal gravity.2

03 of 06

A thin fuel, a pause in the fan

In a Saffire V test on thin cotton fabric, the fan was turned off at about 280 seconds and stayed off for 70 seconds.3 Despite this long interval, the flame grew back when the flow was turned on again.3 It burned for another 150 seconds until the material was consumed.3

Both flamelets and embers were present when imaging stopped, so the authors cannot tell whether the flame survived the next 60 seconds.4 They judge it likely that a surviving flamelet led to the reignition, because embers are typically isolated in a larger area of charred material while flamelets sit at the edge of unburned fuel.5

04 of 06

Still air is not a switch

The Saffire results caution that putting out thin charring fuels at increased oxygen cannot be assumed to happen rapidly in quiescent conditions.6

05 of 06

How big is a big fire

A later flight, Saffire VI, reports fires of 1.5 to 3 kW, which its paper calls a small fire by terrestrial standards.7

06 of 06

What we cannot yet say

What a loaded cargo item would do in such a fire is not covered by the sources we read. Whether the same fan-off behaviour holds for other materials is not covered by the sources we read.

What this story does not show

  • The cotton result is one test on one thin fabric, and the authors judge, but did not observe, why the flame survived.
  • The figure is a simplified drawing of a flame in a duct, not a recording of the flight.
  • No sentence here is a verdict on any material or item.
