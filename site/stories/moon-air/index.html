Does Moon-base air make space fires worse?

How might the air planned for a Moon base change how a fire starts and spreads? This story collects what the sources say, and marks that the whole picture has not yet been looked at together.

Partly data

Each observation comes from a NASA document or a test, but none tested a material at the planned Moon-base air in lunar gravity, so putting them together is our reading.

01 of 06

Different air from the station

The ISS was designed and is operated to approximate an Earth-equivalent atmosphere of 14.7 psia with 21% O2.1 NASA Artemis requirements for sustained missions are targeting an average pressure of 8.2 psi (56.5 kPa) and a 34% oxygen concentration.2

A table of early surface vehicle options lists 8.2 psi with a maximum oxygen concentration of 37% for one architecture, and 9.6 psi with 30% for another.3 Does that air change how a fire starts and spreads?

02 of 06

Less pressure, quicker ignition

In piloted ignition tests of PMMA, reducing the pressure to 58.6 kPa while keeping the oxygen at 21% reduced the ignition time by 17% on average.4 Raising the oxygen at low pressure reduced the ignition time by a further 10%.4

03 of 06

Each material moves its own way

A table of oxygen limits at three total pressures lists silicone at 28% oxygen at 48.2 kPa, 23% at 85.3 kPa and 21% at 101.3 kPa.5 Kevlar and PBI webbings had maximum oxygen concentrations of 37% and 35% oxygen, natural and black, at 8.2 psia.6 The maximum oxygen concentration is expected to depend on sample geometry, thickness, ignition mode, radiant energy, pressure and gravity level, so its conditions should be stated.7

04 of 06

Air data does not carry over

A review of earlier data concludes that results at lower oxygen and higher pressure, such as 21% at 101.4 kPa, cannot be conservatively applied to higher oxygen and lower pressure, such as 30% at 70.3 kPa, despite equivalent partial pressures.8 A white paper says material flammability limits are not expected to meet current requirements under the elevated oxygen (34%) and reduced pressure (8.2 psi) planned for future lunar habitats.9 It adds that these conditions are also expected to affect fire emissions.9

05 of 06

Then there is lunar gravity

Theory and experimental results indicate that oxygen flammability limits in lunar gravity are lower than those measured in normal gravity.10 Some materials that will not burn in normal gravity will burn in weightlessness with a low-speed flow, or in partial gravity such as lunar gravity.11 Essentially all existing test data for flammability of flight materials are from normal-gravity testing, and consequently minimal flammability data exists at varying oxygen concentrations in partial-gravity environments.12

06 of 06

What we cannot yet say

Putting the three effects together, less pressure, more oxygen and lunar gravity, is our reading of separate results. None of the sources we read tests a material at the planned Moon-base air in lunar gravity.

What this story does not show

  • No design of a Moon base is given here: the 34% oxygen and 8.2 psi are a NASA target for sustained missions.
  • No result here was measured on the Moon.
  • The expectation that planned conditions fall short of current requirements is not a test result.
  • No sentence here is a verdict on any material or item.
