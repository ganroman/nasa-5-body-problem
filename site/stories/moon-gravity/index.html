What burns on the Moon?

How does a flame behave in the gravity of the Moon? This story follows what ground tests with simulated lunar gravity say, and marks that none of them was run on the Moon.

Partly data

Each observation comes from a drop tower, centrifuge, aircraft or model, none from the Moon itself, so the picture of a Moon fire is not yet measured.

01 of 05

A gravity in between

Theory and experimental results indicate that oxygen flammability limits in lunar gravity are lower than those measured in normal gravity.1 A NASA Glenn poster reports increasing evidence from 5.18-second centrifuge tests at the drop facility that materials have lower oxygen limits of flammability in lunar gravity than in Earth gravity.2

02 of 05

What burns that did not

Theoretical and experimental results have demonstrated that some materials that will not burn in normal gravity will burn in weightlessness with a low-speed flow, or in partial gravity such as lunar gravity.3 NASA's reduced-pressure report reasons that a flame can go out if cabin airflow is high enough, and that this happens more readily on Earth because higher gravity causes higher flow velocities.4 So a material that self-extinguishes in Earth gravity may continue to burn in lunar gravity.4

03 of 05

Slower, in one test

In the first extended-duration burn in simulated lunar gravity, the flame base of a fabric spread at about 0.9 mm/s versus 1.5 mm/s in the 1-g comparison, and the flame tip at 1.1 versus 2.8 mm/s.5 So in this test the speed of spread was lower in lunar gravity, which is why we do not read "worse" into every number.

04 of 05

What people expect

A 2004 paper estimates that on the Moon or Mars (0.17 g and 0.38 g) buoyant flows will be greater than 20 cm/s, so concurrent flame spread will be viable.6 A 2026 abstract says that a partial-gravity fire in an extraterrestrial habitat is a real hazard that is expected to be substantially worse than in 0-g and potentially worse than even 1-g.7

Essentially all existing test data for flammability of flight materials are from 1-g testing.8 A 2021 NASA presentation says additional work is needed to establish a partial gravity flammability acceptance criterion.9

05 of 05

What we cannot yet say

No source we read reports a fire lit on the Moon. How a fire in a Moon-base habitat compares with one on the station is not covered by the sources we read.

What this story does not show

  • No result here was measured on the Moon.
  • The lunar-gravity results come from drop-facility and centrifuge tests, not from the Moon.
  • The speed result comes from the first extended-duration burn in simulated lunar gravity.
  • The statement that such a fire is expected to be worse is an expectation, not a measured result.
  • No sentence here is a verdict on any material or item.
