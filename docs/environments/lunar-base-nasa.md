# NASA lunar surface base: systems, logistics, and crew

Research reference for the **Flame in Freefall** challenge · Team Five-Body Problem · 14 November 2026

A lunar surface base would be a small research outpost with living space, utilities, vehicles, and supply facilities. NASA plans to develop it near the lunar South Pole, adding capabilities over multiple missions. It is distinct from Gateway, which was designed to orbit the Moon. [NASA Moon Base FAQ](https://www.nasa.gov/moon-base-frequently-asked-questions/)

This document separates NASA's published plans from a functional breakdown for our research. The tables are not a final floor plan, cargo manifest, or assigned crew roster.

## 1. Main blocks and their purposes

“Blocks” here means functions. Several could share one physical module, and the base would not have every capability from its first mission.

| Block | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Living habitat** | Sleeping, eating, hygiene, exercise, and medical care in a pressurised environment where astronauts can live without spacesuits. |
| **Life support and thermal control** | Supplies breathable air, removes carbon dioxide, manages water and waste, and keeps people and equipment at suitable temperatures. |
| **Airlock and suit servicing** | Allows entry and exit without emptying the habitat's air. Supports suit inspection, charging, maintenance, and dust control. |
| **Science and maintenance workspace** | Examines and packages samples, operates experiments, and supports equipment repairs. |
| **Storage and logistics** | Stores food, spare parts, tools, gases, and scientific supplies; manages incoming cargo and outgoing samples. |
| **Power system** | Generates, stores, and distributes electricity to habitats, instruments, vehicles, and communications equipment. |
| **Communications and control** | Connects astronauts, robots, and Earth; monitors equipment and supports navigation. |
| **Surface mobility** | Open rovers carry astronauts wearing suits. Pressurised rovers provide a breathable mobile workspace for longer expeditions. |
| **Landing and cargo-handling area** | Receives people and equipment and supports departure. Landers are visiting transport vehicles. |
| **Later resource-processing equipment** | Tests ways to use lunar materials so future missions may need fewer supplies from Earth. Local production should not be assumed available for early crews. |

NASA groups the major planned systems into mobility, communications/navigation, habitation, power, and logistics. The table expands those categories into everyday activities relevant to our research. [NASA Moon Base Systems](https://www.nasa.gov/moonbase-systems/)

## 2. Deliveries between Earth and the base

The following describes expected operational needs, not confirmed shipment quantities or manifests.

| Direction | What travels | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Earth → Moon: construction** | Habitats, power equipment, communications systems, rovers, robots, and scientific instruments | Establish and expand the base. |
| **Earth → Moon: crew** | Astronauts and their mission equipment | Operate the base and conduct surface expeditions. |
| **Earth → Moon: regular supplies** | Food, water and breathing-gas replenishment, clothing, hygiene products, and medicines | Keep the crew alive and healthy. Recycling reduces replenishment needs but does not eliminate them. |
| **Earth → Moon: maintenance** | Spare parts, filters, batteries, cables, tools, suit components, and emergency equipment | Replace worn or failed equipment. |
| **Earth → Moon: research** | Experiments, drills, sample containers, and packaging | Conduct investigations and protect collected material. |
| **Moon → Earth: crew** | Returning astronauts | Complete crew rotations and support recovery and medical assessment. |
| **Moon → Earth: scientific material** | Selected rocks, lunar soil, and carefully preserved samples | Enable analysis using Earth laboratories. |
| **Moon → Earth: selected hardware** | Experiment specimens or failed components worth examining | Understand performance and improve future designs. |
| **Moon → Earth: information** | Measurements, images, video, equipment status, and research results | Most information is transmitted electronically rather than carried on a cargo flight. |

Heavy infrastructure generally stays on the Moon. Returning bulk lunar resources to Earth is not an assumption needed for this research; early local resource use would primarily support lunar activities.

Sample return is an explicit Artemis science activity. NASA's current Artemis IV description uses a lander between the surface and lunar orbit, then Orion for Earth return. Deliveries of infrastructure can use separate uncrewed missions. [NASA Artemis IV](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/artemis-iv/) · [NASA Moon Base FAQ](https://www.nasa.gov/moon-base-frequently-asked-questions/)

**Illustrative cargo journey:** astronauts take empty sample containers from Earth, collect lunar rocks, seal and label them, and transfer the containers into the Earth-return spacecraft. This illustrates the process; it is not a confirmed mission manifest.

## 3. Crew size and responsibilities

There is no resident lunar-base crew today. Crew size and stay duration depend on the stage of development.

| Reference | Crew and duration | How to interpret it |
|---|---|---|
| **Current Artemis IV description** | Four astronauts travel in Orion; two descend to the surface for approximately a week. | An early surface expedition. Four travelling to the Moon does not mean four living on its surface. |
| **Earlier NASA surface-habitat study** | Up to four surface residents for 30–60 days. | A useful research reference, not a confirmed staffing specification for the current base. |

Sources: [NASA Artemis IV](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/artemis-iv/) · [NASA, Artemis Deep Space Habitation study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220000245)

For an **illustrative four-person expedition**, responsibilities could be divided as follows. This is a research example, not NASA's assigned crew structure.

| Responsibility | Typical work |
|---|---|
| **Commander / operations lead** | Coordinates activities, communications, and emergency decisions. |
| **Systems engineer** | Maintains power, life support, communications, and habitat equipment. |
| **Science lead** | Plans fieldwork, collects samples, and operates experiments. |
| **Surface operations specialist** | Supports rovers, suits, cargo handling, and external maintenance. |

These responsibilities overlap. Crew members need cross-training, including medical and emergency duties, while specialists on Earth provide additional support. The four rows should not be read as requiring a separate person for every function on a smaller expedition.

## 4. Relevance to Flame in Freefall

The direct connection to our fire-safety research is the habitat, pressurised rover, airlock, and equipment brought inside: wiring, batteries, clothing, plastics, and packaging. Their actual operating conditions matter, including oxygen concentration, total pressure, airflow, and gravity.

The outdoor lunar vacuum does not support ordinary combustion using ambient oxygen. That does not make all external equipment free of fire or thermal hazards: batteries and systems carrying their own oxidiser need separate assessment.

Our [research brief](../research-brief.md) identifies the central evidence gap: PSI microgravity results do not directly establish fire behaviour in lunar gravity or a different cabin atmosphere. The challenge's elevated-oxygen habitat scenario should be labelled as a scenario, not treated as a universal specification for every base module, suit, or rover.

For example, moving a battery-powered instrument from an external cargo carrier into a breathable habitat changes the surrounding conditions. Its suitability needs evidence for that destination; the fact that it travelled safely does not establish its fire safety inside the habitat. This is an illustrative rule, not a reported test result.

## 5. Limits of this research

This reference does not calculate cargo masses, consumable quantities, power demand, or emergency reserves. Those depend on crew size, mission duration, recycling performance, and selected hardware. The final base layout, module atmosphere specifications, and long-term crew roster are not established by the sources reviewed here.
