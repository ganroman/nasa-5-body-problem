# Lunar Gateway: modules, logistics, crew, and comparison with the ISS

Research reference for the **Flame in Freefall** challenge · Team Five-Body Problem · 14 November 2026

Gateway was designed as a small station orbiting the Moon: a place for astronauts to live, conduct research, and transfer between Earth-return spacecraft and lunar landers. It would remain in orbit; a separate base would stand on the lunar surface.

## Program status

**On 24 March 2026, NASA announced its intention to pause Gateway in its current form and redirect effort toward a lunar surface base.** This document describes the **previously planned Gateway architecture**, not a confirmed deployment plan. Some NASA overview pages still describe the earlier plan and carry an update notice. [NASA’s announcement](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-unveils-initiatives-to-achieve-americas-national-space-policy/)

## 1. Modules and their purposes

Not every element is a room: some are external equipment attached to the station.

| Element | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **PPE — Power and Propulsion Element** | Solar electricity, communications, and thrusters to maintain or change the station’s orbit and orientation. |
| **HALO — Habitation and Logistics Outpost** | Initial living and working space; also the station’s command centre, with power distribution, computers, and temperature control. |
| **Lunar I-Hab — International Habitation Module** | Additional living quarters, sleeping and dining space, research facilities, and life-support equipment. |
| **Lunar View** | Storage for supplies, refuelling of the PPE, and windows for observation. |
| **Lunar Link** | Communications equipment connecting Gateway with landers, rovers, and other spacecraft around the Moon. Mounted on HALO. |
| **Crew and Science Airlock** | Lets astronauts and experiments move between the pressurised interior and the vacuum outside without depressurising the whole station. |
| **Canadarm3** | External robotic system for inspection, maintenance, and handling equipment, including during periods without astronauts. |

NASA, ESA, Japan, Canada, and the UAE were contributing these systems. [NASA’s component overview](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/gateway-about/)

**Orion, lunar landers, and cargo spacecraft would dock temporarily.** They are transport vehicles, rather than permanent Gateway modules.

## 2. Transport between Earth, Gateway, and the Moon

The following describes cargo categories and their purposes—not a fixed mission packing list.

| Route | People or cargo | Why it goes there |
|---|---|---|
| **Earth → Gateway** | Astronauts aboard Orion; station modules; food, water and atmospheric supplies; clothing, medical supplies, spare parts, tools, and experiments | Build and maintain the station, support its crew, and prepare lunar expeditions. |
| **Gateway → lunar surface** | Astronauts aboard a lunar lander, with surface tools, experiments, sample containers, and supplies for their stay | Explore the Moon and carry out scientific work. |
| **Earth → lunar surface directly** | Robotic instruments, rovers, power and communications equipment; later, habitats and heavier infrastructure | Establish surface capabilities. These deliveries do not all need to pass through Gateway. |
| **Lunar surface → Gateway** | Returning astronauts, sealed rock and soil samples, and selected equipment or experiments | Transfer the crew and scientifically valuable material to the Earth-return vehicle. |
| **Gateway → Earth** | Astronauts aboard Orion, selected lunar samples, and research samples or small items needing examination | Bring people home and allow detailed laboratory analysis on Earth. |

NASA’s Gateway logistics plan included delivery of both cargo kept inside a pressurised spacecraft and equipment carried outside it. Its science plans explicitly included transferring lunar samples for return to Earth. The newer surface-base plan emphasises direct deliveries of rovers, instruments, and infrastructure. [Gateway logistics](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-awards-artemis-contract-for-gateway-logistics-services/) · [Sample-return capabilities](https://www.nasa.gov/gateway-capabilities/) · [Surface-base plan](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-unveils-initiatives-to-achieve-americas-national-space-policy/)

**Illustrative expedition:** a Gateway-based expedition could carry empty sample containers from Earth, take them down in a lander, collect lunar rocks, bring the sealed containers back to Gateway, and transfer them into Orion for Earth return. This illustrates the transport sequence; it is not a confirmed mission manifest.

Two distinctions matter:

- **Measurements, photographs, and video can be transmitted electronically.** Physical samples need a spacecraft.
- **Shipping Moon-produced water, oxygen, or fuel to Gateway would require additional mining, processing, and transport infrastructure.** Treat that as a future scenario, not an established supply chain.

## 3. Crew and daily work

The earlier design accommodated **up to four astronauts**, with stays of roughly **30 days or longer**, depending on the assembled modules and available supplies. Gateway would **not be permanently occupied**; remote control and autonomous systems would operate it between visits. [NASA Gateway FAQ](https://www.nasa.gov/gateway-frequently-asked-questions/) · [Habitation design](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/gateway-halo-a-deep-space-home/)

The astronauts’ work would include:

- Operating and maintaining the station.
- Running experiments.
- Preparing lunar landers and surface equipment.
- Handling cargo and samples.
- Performing spacewalks when required.

There is no current resident Gateway crew. “Four astronauts” describes its intended capacity, not four assigned permanent residents.

## 4. Comparison with the ISS

| Aspect | ISS | Gateway’s earlier design |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Low Earth orbit, roughly 400 km above Earth | An elongated lunar orbit, called a *near-rectilinear halo orbit* |
| **Orbit duration** | About 90 minutes | About 6.5 days |
| **Main role** | Continuously occupied research laboratory | Lunar expedition staging point, research station, and deep-space technology test site |
| **Crew** | Normally seven; continuously occupied | Up to four in early configurations; occupied during visits |
| **Typical stay** | Several months | Around a month or longer, depending on configuration |
| **Scale** | Large laboratory complex | Much smaller, with fewer living and working spaces |
| **Gravity inside** | Microgravity: people and objects float | Also microgravity—not the Moon’s surface gravity |
| **Radiation** | Some protection from Earth’s magnetic field | Greater exposure beyond that protective environment |
| **Return to Earth** | A docked return spacecraft can reach Earth on the scale of hours | Return requires a journey measured in days, depending on trajectory |
| **Operation without crew** | Crew normally available to investigate problems | Long unattended periods are part of the operating concept |

Sources: [NASA ISS facts](https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station/space-station-facts-and-figures/) · [NASA Gateway FAQ](https://www.nasa.gov/gateway-frequently-asked-questions/).

## 5. Relevance to our fire-safety challenge

**Gateway and the ISS are microgravity habitats, while a lunar surface habitat experiences about one-sixth of Earth’s gravity.** That changes how hot gases and smoke move. ISS combustion research can inform Gateway studies, but applying it to a Moon base requires accounting for partial gravity and the actual cabin atmosphere.

For our research, we should **keep oxygen concentration and cabin pressure separate for each environment**. The elevated-oxygen lunar-habitat scenario in our [research brief](../research-brief.md) should not automatically become a Gateway specification.

These are implications for interpreting the research, not evidence that a particular material or fire-safety system is qualified for Gateway or a lunar base.

## Limits and open questions

- Detailed cabin-atmosphere specifications and mission cargo masses were not verified in this research.
- The transport table identifies categories and purposes, not quantities or a confirmed mission manifest.
- The earlier Gateway design remains useful as a research reference, but must not be presented as NASA’s current committed deployment plan.
