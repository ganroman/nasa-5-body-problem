# China's planned lunar base: ILRS

Research notes for the **Flame in Freefall** challenge · 14 November 2026

The **International Lunar Research Station (ILRS)** is a planned lunar research facility led by China with Russia and other international partners. This note focuses on its future permanent infrastructure, supplies and crew requirements. It excludes China's first crewed lunar landing mission.

**Permanent infrastructure does not necessarily mean permanent human occupation.** Public plans describe long-term robotic operation with short human visits. A basic station near the lunar south pole is targeted around **2035**, followed by expansion. Published expansion dates differ: a September 2024 account describes a network by about 2050, while an April 2025 account refers to an extended model in the 2040s. These are planning targets, not completed facilities or guaranteed dates. [1][2][3]

## 1. Base blocks and their purposes

Official descriptions identify facilities **on the lunar surface, in lunar orbit and on Earth**, including power, command and information systems, and lunar surface transportation. [4]

The table below translates those plans into functional groups. It is **not a finalized architectural blueprint or an official module manifest**. Several functions could share one structure, and many scientific instruments could operate outdoors without a pressurized laboratory.

| Functional block | Purpose | Evidence and uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Power and thermal control | Supply electricity and keep equipment within operating temperatures. | Power infrastructure is announced; the final power mix and thermal-control configuration are not established by the reviewed sources. |
| Communications and operations | Connect surface equipment, orbital infrastructure and Earth; coordinate robots and experiments. | Command and information infrastructure is announced. |
| Science facilities | Study lunar geology, astronomy, the space environment and fundamental physics. | These are announced scientific objectives; a detailed laboratory layout is not established. |
| Surface transport and robotics | Move equipment, explore surrounding terrain and collect samples. | Surface transportation is announced; the final vehicle fleet is not established. |
| Local-resource processing | Test producing useful supplies or construction materials from lunar resources. | Resource-use research is planned; dependable industrial production or self-sufficiency should not be assumed. |
| Crew accommodation and life support | Support breathing, sleeping, eating, hygiene, medical care and protection during visits. | Human visits are planned; habitat configuration, capacity and cabin atmosphere are not established by the reviewed sources. |
| Airlocks, maintenance and storage | Support outside work, manage dust, service equipment and hold supplies. | These are engineering requirements for crewed operations, rather than a confirmed list of ILRS modules. |
| Cargo reception and return | Receive supplies and enable crew and sample departures. | These are necessary logistics functions; a routine transport schedule and cargo-handling design are not established. |

The announced science objectives include lunar geology, lunar-based astronomy, observations of the Sun–Earth–Moon environment, fundamental science experiments and use of local resources. [3]

## 2. What must travel between Earth and the base?

The following table describes **expected engineering needs, not a published ILRS cargo manifest**. Quantities depend on equipment choices, crew size, visit duration, recycling performance and whether local production becomes reliable.

| Flow | Main contents | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Earth → base: construction | Power systems, communications equipment, robots, instruments, habitat hardware, tools and cables. | Establish and expand the base. |
| Earth → base: replenishment | Food, replacement electronics, batteries, filters, seals, repair materials, medical supplies and experiment consumables. | Keep equipment operating and support crew visits. |
| Earth → base: life-support supplies and reserves | Water and breathing gases, with imports potentially reduced by recycling and dependable local production. | Maintain reliable supplies for people. |
| Earth → base: new capabilities | New instruments, resource-processing equipment and upgraded robots. | Extend research and improve local operations. |
| Earth → base: transport systems | Crew and cargo vehicles carrying the propellant needed for their missions. | Deliver and return people and payloads; local fuel production should not be assumed. |
| Base → Earth: scientific cargo | Selected rocks, soil, drill cores, experimental specimens and potentially volatile-bearing samples. | Perform analyses unavailable on the Moon; some samples may need special temperature control. |
| Base → Earth: selected equipment | Failed components or exposed materials worth examining. | Investigate failures and long-term degradation. |
| Both directions: people | Visiting crews and their personal equipment. | Conduct research, maintenance and construction, then return home. |
| Base → Earth: transmitted results | Scientific measurements, imagery, equipment-health records and crew observations. | Deliver research and operational information by radio rather than as physical cargo. |

Lunar sample return is included in published ILRS research planning. [5] Routine commercial export of lunar minerals to Earth is **not established by the sources reviewed**.

Local resource use primarily reduces imports. For example, if water extraction becomes reliable, it could supply some local demand. Until then, crew planning needs a dependable alternative. This example illustrates the dependency; it is not evidence that ILRS already has a working water-production system.

## 3. What is the crew?

**The reviewed sources do not establish a fixed ILRS crew size, named crew, nationality allocation, visit duration or permanent staffing schedule.** The published operating model is long-term robotic operation with short human visits, supported by teams on Earth. [1]

During visits, people would need to cover the following responsibilities. These are an engineering interpretation of the work, not announced crew positions.

| Responsibility | Work at the base |
|---|---|
| Operations and emergency response | Coordinate activities, manage failures and prepare evacuation. |
| Engineering and maintenance | Service power, communications, robots and life-support equipment. |
| Science and fieldwork | Collect samples, operate experiments and document results. |
| Medical and life-support oversight | Monitor health, cabin conditions, water and breathing-gas supplies. |

These are **roles, not four confirmed crew members**. A small crew would combine responsibilities and require cross-training. No specific headcount should be presented as a confirmed ILRS requirement on the basis of this research.

## 4. Relevance to Flame in Freefall

For the challenge, the applicable setting is **a future ILRS crewed habitat during an occupied period**. This describes the fire-safety context without implying that continuous human occupation has been announced.

Distinguish equipment outside in lunar vacuum from items transported, stored or operated inside a breathable cabin. For example, an astronaut bringing a battery-powered drill inside also brings its battery, plastics, wiring and accumulated dust into that cabin. Relevant fire-safety evidence must account for the item's configuration and operating conditions. This is an illustrative scenario, not a reported ILRS incident or test.

The lunar surface has approximately one-sixth Earth's gravity. Microgravity combustion results can inform research, but they do not directly validate performance under lunar gravity. Cabin oxygen concentration, pressure, ventilation and material configuration also affect whether experimental evidence applies.

Do not label a generic exploration-habitat oxygen concentration or pressure as an announced Chinese specification. No finalized ILRS cabin atmosphere was established by the sources reviewed. Rocket and propellant combustion remain outside the project's stated PSI research scope; their presence in the logistics table does not extend that scope.
